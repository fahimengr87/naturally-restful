## Answer 1 — "Is it safe to take melatonin every night?"
Short-term: yes, remarkably safe at correct doses — which almost nobody takes. The effective dose in jet-lag and shift-work research is 0.5–1 mg; most bottles sell 5–10 mg, 5–10x the studied dose, which mostly adds morning grogginess. Melatonin is a clock signal (it shifts sleep *timing*), not a sedative. For plain stress-related insomnia, it underperforms its reputation. If used nightly long-term, the honest caveat is simply that multi-year daily-use data is thin — cycles or as-needed use is the conservative pattern. Anyone on blood thinners should talk to a pharmacist first (interactions are documented).

## Answer 2 — "Which magnesium is best for sleep?"
Glycinate (or bisglycinate), and it isn't close for the bedtime job. It's chelated — gentle on the gut — and carries the best sleep-trial support of the common forms (modest, consistent improvements at 200–400 mg elemental). Citrate absorbs fine but draws water into the intestines — it's literally sold as a laxative, which is self-defeating at 10 p.m. Oxide is nearly worthless (~4% absorption). Threonate's premium buys cognition hypotheses built mostly on rat studies, not sleep. Judge any of them at 2–4 weeks, not night one — magnesium is a slow regulator, not a sleeping pill.

## Answer 3 — "Why do I wake up at 3 a.m. every night?"
The most under-diagnosed culprit is alcohol — it sedates early, then rebounds as it metabolizes; the "3 a.m. mystery" is often just metabolized wine. Next: stress-cortisol timing, blood-sugar dips from late-evening sugar, and (if waking with gasping or headaches) sleep apnea — which needs a doctor, not supplements. Age lightens sleep architecture too. Quick wins people skip: caffeine's 5–6 hour half-life (a 4 p.m. coffee is still quarter-active at midnight), a genuinely cool room, and getting out of bed after 20 minutes awake to retrain the brain's bed-sleep association. Glycine (3 g) and magnesium glycinate are the evidence-friendly supplements *for this specific pattern* — melatonin is not (wrong tool: it shifts sleep timing, not maintenance).

## Answer 4 — "Does ashwagandha actually work for stress?"
Yes — it's one of the better-studied stress supplements, with honest caveats. The key 2012 trial (Chandrasekhar, KSM-66 600 mg/day) cut average cortisol ~28% in chronically stressed adults; meta-analyses since point the same direction for perceived stress and anxiety. The form matters enormously: standardized extracts (KSM-66 root-only 5%, or Sensoril ~10%) have the trial records; random "root powder 500 mg" jars are agricultural roulette. Give it the full 6–8 weeks the trials measured. Hard exclusions: pregnancy (miscarriage risk — clearest no in supplements), thyroid disease without a doctor, autoimmune conditions, sedatives. Occasional mild GI upset otherwise.

## Answer 5 — "Are sleep supplements a scam?"
Mostly no, partially yes — the honest split: A few have genuine human-trial support for modest effects: magnesium (glycinate form), glycine at its actual 3 g trial dose, low-dose melatonin (0.5–1 mg, timing purposes), ashwagandha for stress-driven trouble, L-theanine for a racing mind. "Modest" is the honest adjective — none rival prescription sleep medicine or CBT-I. The scam tier: GABA pills (mostly can't cross into the brain — you're paying for the diagram, not the effect), "adaptogen" drinks at half-doses, gummy products at 5–20x melatonin doses with sugar on top, and anything promising to "cure insomnia." The boring truth: supplements sit ON TOP of sleep hygiene (consistent schedule, morning light, caffeine curfew, cool room) — not instead of it.
