Few supplements have had a run like ashwagandha. An ayurvedic root that suddenly shows up everywhere: stress gummies, "adaptogen" coffee, focus drinks. And when something gets that popular, the honest question isn't "does it work?" but **"how well, for whom, and what's the catch?"**

I've spent more evenings than I should admit reading ashwagandha trials. Here's what I found.

## The trials, and what they actually measured

The most-cited study (Chandrasekhar et al., 2012) is worth understanding properly because it's the one everyone quotes:

64 chronically stressed adults. 300 mg of a standardized root extract (KSM-66), twice daily, for 60 days. The results:

- Perceived stress scores dropped substantially versus placebo
- Average **cortisol fell around 28%** — and this is the part I find genuinely impressive. Not a mood questionnaire. A measurable hormone change in the bloodstream.
- Sleep quality and self-reported vitality improved

Follow-up trials and several meta-analyses point the same direction. Reduced stress and anxiety scores, modest cortisol reductions, better subjective sleep. Mostly in stressed-but-otherwise-healthy adults.

Now the caveats. Because there are always caveats, and pretending otherwise is how bad health content happens:

**The samples are small.** Many positive trials run 40–100 people. Promising? Absolutely. Rock-solid? Not yet — and I won't pretend otherwise.

**Publication bias is real.** Small supplement trials with positive results get published; duds vanish into a drawer. Meta-analyses try to correct for this and still find an effect. Just a realistically modest one.

**Extract identity matters enormously.** "Ashwagandha" isn't one substance. KSM-66 (root-only) and Sensoril (root + leaf) are the two standardized extracts with actual trial records behind them. A random powder blend with a leafy label and a meditation silhouette on the jar? That's not what the studies tested. I see these on shelves constantly and it frustrates me.

## What you can realistically expect

Based on the evidence: a **modest reduction in perceived stress over 4–8 weeks**. Not dramatic. Not overnight. The kind of change you notice in hindsight — "huh, I've been less on edge this month."

Doses in the trials: **300–600 mg/day of a standardized extract**, usually split morning and evening. Effects were measured at 6–8 weeks, not 6 days. If a brand promises results in three days, they're selling you impatience.

## Who must not take this

I'm going to be direct, because this section matters more than the rest of the article:

**Pregnancy: hard no.** Ashwagandha is associated with miscarriage risk. This is the clearest contraindication in the entire supplement world. If you're pregnant, close this tab.

**Thyroid conditions: doctor first.** Ashwagandha can push thyroid hormone activity upward. For hypothyroid readers, it may potentiate medication (doses drift). For hyperthyroidism, it can make things actively worse. There are case reports of ashwagandha-triggered thyrotoxicosis. This isn't theoretical — it's in the literature.

**Autoimmune disease or immunosuppressants:** ashwagandha stimulates immune activity — the *opposite* of what immunosuppressive treatment does. Risk of flares.

**Sedatives, blood pressure, and blood sugar medication:** it can amplify all three. Pharmacist check, two minutes.

## Bottom line

Ashwagandha clears a bar most stress supplements never even approach: multiple small human trials showing consistent, biologically measurable effects. If your sleep trouble is obviously stress-driven, it's the most defensible "calm" supplement to try — from a standardized extract (KSM-66 or Sensoril), at trial doses, with a doctor's sign-off if any of the flags above apply to you.

And if your stress is mild and occasional? A walk and a consistent bedtime remain undefeated.

**Key studies:** Chandrasekhar et al., *Indian J Psychol Med* 2012 (KSM-66 600 mg/day: cortisol −27.9%) · Salve et al., *Cureus* 2019 (300 mg BID: sleep onset + quality) · Pratte et al., *J Ayurveda Integr Med* 2014 meta-analysis.
