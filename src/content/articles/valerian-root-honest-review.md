Valerian has been Europe's sleep herb since ancient Greece, and modern science has spent fifty years failing to decide whether it works. That's not a typo — the research genuinely conflicts, and understanding *why* is more useful than pretending the average is an answer.

## What the studies show when you stack them

Meta-analyses of valerian sleep studies land on the same awkward sentence every time: *small improvements in subjective sleep onset latency and quality, inconsistent across trials, high variability between products.* Some well-run trials show real benefit; equally well-run trials show nothing. No pattern of results you'd call reliable.

## Why the mess? Three honest reasons

1. **The product varies wildly.** Valerian preparations differ in valerenic acid content (the marker compound), root vs extract, freshness — and aged valerian loses potency. Studies tested materially different things.
2. **The effect, if real, is slow.** Some trials suggest valerian needs 2+ weeks of nightly use — many studies ran one night or one week, structurally set up to miss it.
3. **Expectation effects are strong in sleep studies.** Valerian's characteristic smell makes blinding hard — participants often know they got the herb.

## The one use with decent support

**Menopausal hot-flush-related sleep trouble** has some of valerian's better-controlled positive results, and **situational sleep anxiety** shows mild benefit in a few trials. For general insomnia, the evidence ranks below [magnesium](/articles/magnesium-before-bed-timing-and-dosage), [glycine](/articles/glycine-for-sleep-the-3-gram-dose), and [ashwagandha](/articles/ashwagandha-for-stress-what-the-research-says) — which is why it doesn't make our [top picks](/best-sleep-supplements).

## If you try it: the protocol that gives it its best shot

300–600 mg of a **standardized extract** (0.3–0.5% valerenic acid), nightly, **for a full month** before judging. Caution: don't combine with sedatives or alcohol (additive drowsiness), and a small minority report valerian *stimulating* rather than sedating them — a known quirk worth knowing on night one.
