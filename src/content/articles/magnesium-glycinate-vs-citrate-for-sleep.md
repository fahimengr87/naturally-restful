Magnesium is the first supplement most sleep researchers mention when asked about minerals and rest — and the first thing supplement brands exploit. Walk into any pharmacy and you'll find magnesium oxide, citrate, glycinate, threonate, bisglycinate, and at least three proprietary blends with moons on the label.

This article compares the two forms people actually ask about — **glycinate and citrate** — and answers the only question that matters: which one supports sleep better?

## What magnesium actually does for sleep

Magnesium regulates hundreds of processes in the body, including the function of GABA — the neurotransmitter your brain uses to quiet neural activity. That's the same system many prescription sleep drugs target, far more bluntly.

The research picture, honestly stated: **human trials on magnesium and sleep show modest, fairly consistent improvements in sleep quality and sleep time** — mostly in people with low magnesium intake or heightened stress. It is not a sedative. If your sleep is ruined by untreated sleep apnea, no form of magnesium fixes that.

The form you take matters mainly for one practical reason: **how much magnesium actually gets absorbed, and how your gut reacts to it.**

## Magnesium glycinate: the bedtime form

Glycinate (often sold as bisglycinate) is magnesium bound to glycine, an amino acid that has its own mild calming research behind it. Two properties make it the standard recommendation for sleep:

1. **It's chelated**, which protects it through the digestive tract and improves absorption compared to cheap inorganic forms like oxide.
2. **It's gentle.** This is the deciding factor. Glycinate rarely causes the digestive urgency that other forms do — exactly what you want in something taken at 10 p.m.

The glycine bonus isn't huge, but small trials on glycine alone (around 3 g) showed faster sleep onset and better subjective rest. Glycinate gives you a fraction of that dose, bound to the mineral.

**Typical dose:** 200–400 mg of elemental magnesium, an hour or two before bed. Give it 2–4 weeks.

## Magnesium citrate: good mineral, wrong shift

Citrate is actually a *decent* absorber — noticeably better than oxide, and usually cheaper than glycinate. If glycinate didn't exist, citrate would be a reasonable daily magnesium source.

But citrate draws water into the intestines, which is why pharmacy shelves stock it as a **laxative** — the bottles in the colored boxes aren't in the sleep aisle. At sleep-relevant doses, plenty of people get loose stools or urgent morning bathroom trips. A supplement that wakes you up at 6 a.m. to visit the bathroom has an obvious flaw as a sleep aid.

**Verdict:** citrate is fine for general magnesium intake if your gut tolerates it. For sleep specifically, glycinate wins on tolerance, and that's not a close call.

## Who should skip both

- **People with kidney disease** — impaired kidneys can't clear excess magnesium; this is the one hard safety line. Talk to your doctor first.
- **Anyone on certain antibiotics or bisphosphonates** — magnesium blocks absorption of some drugs; separate doses by several hours or ask a pharmacist.
- **People expecting a sedative.** Expect "slightly better sleep quality over weeks," not lights-out.

## The bottom line

For sleep support, **magnesium glycinate at 200–400 mg is the better choice of the two** — same mineral, gentler delivery, no 6 a.m. emergencies. Buy from brands with third-party testing (NSF, USP, or Informed Choice marks), because magnesium supplements are one of the categories where label accuracy drifts most.

And keep expectations calibrated: magnesium is a modest helper to good sleep habits, not a replacement for them.
