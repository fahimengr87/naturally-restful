Melatonin is the most-purchased sleep supplement in the world. And after reading the dose-finding literature, I'm convinced most people are taking it wrong — not dangerously wrong, just *wastefully* wrong. Doses several times larger than the research supports, at times that don't match the hormone's actual job. Let me explain what I mean.

## What melatonin actually does (and doesn't do)

Here's the first thing to understand, and it changes everything else: melatonin doesn't sedate you. Your pineal gland releases it in the evening as a **biological time signal**: "night is starting." It shifts the *timing* of your sleep — it doesn't knock you out.

This is why melatonin shines for jet lag and schedule problems, and why it underwhelms for garden-variety "can't sleep" stress. If your problem is "my body clock is in the wrong timezone," melatonin is the right tool. If your problem is "my mind won't stop replaying that conversation from 2019," that's a different animal entirely.

## The dose problem — and it's a big one

Jet-lag and shift-work trials consistently find benefits at **0.5–1 mg**. Above that, effects on sleep timing don't meaningfully increase — but next-morning grogginess does. Read that again: taking more doesn't help more, it just adds side effects.

Now go look at your bottle. I'll wait.

It probably says 5 mg or 10 mg, doesn't it? US melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, which means doses are set by marketing departments, not pharmacology. There's no regulatory cap, so brands compete on "more is stronger" shelf logic — even though the science says otherwise.

And it gets worse: independent analyses of melatonin products have found actual content ranging from **−83% to +478%** of the label claim. Let that sink in. Your "3 mg" gummy might contain 0.5 mg or 14 mg. This is why I recommend tablets from third-party-tested brands over gummies — not because gummies are inherently bad, but because the dose chaos in this category is genuinely concerning.

## Timing: where most people get it wrong

Because melatonin is a clock signal, *when* you take it outranks how much you take. Here's the table that should come with every bottle but never does:

| Situation | What to actually do |
|---|---|
| Falling asleep too late (delayed rhythm) | 0.5–1 mg, **3–4 hours before** desired bedtime. Yes, that early. |
| Eastbound jet lag | 0.5–1 mg at local target bedtime, starting the evening before travel |
| Night-shift work | Strategically, with medical guidance — genuinely tricky |
| Ordinary stress insomnia | Honestly? Modest evidence. Sleep-hygiene work first. |

The common habit — popping 10 mg at midnight after lying awake for an hour — is the least useful deployment possible. Too much, too late, for the wrong problem.

## Safety notes (brief, but important)

- **Short-term adult use is well tolerated.** Headaches and morning fog at high doses are the main complaints.
- **Kids and teens:** growing use, but pediatrician involvement is non-negotiable. Not a gummy-brand-Instagram decision.
- **Interactions:** blood thinners (warfarin especially), immunosuppressants, diabetes medication, contraceptives. If any apply, ask a pharmacist — it takes two minutes.
- **Pregnancy/nursing:** not enough data. Skip it.

## What to do instead (if melatonin isn't the answer)

For ordinary stress-related sleep trouble, the better-studied options on this site — [magnesium glycinate](/articles/magnesium-glycinate-vs-citrate-for-sleep), [ashwagandha](/articles/ashwagandha-for-stress-what-the-research-says), and boring sleep-schedule discipline — earn their place first. Melatonin is a timing tool, not a sleep-force tool.
