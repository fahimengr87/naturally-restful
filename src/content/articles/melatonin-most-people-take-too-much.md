Melatonin is the most-purchased sleep supplement in the world, and most people take it wrong. Not dangerously wrong — melatonin is remarkably safe short-term — but *inefficiently* wrong: doses several times larger than the research supports, taken at times that don't match the hormone's actual job.

## What melatonin actually does

Melatonin doesn't sedate you. Your pineal gland releases it in the evening as a **biological time signal**: "night is starting." It shifts the *timing* of your sleep — which is why it shines for jet lag and schedule problems and underwhelms for garden-variety insomnia.

That mechanism explains everything else in this article.

## The dose problem

Jet-lag and shift-work trials consistently find benefits at **0.5–1 mg**. Above that, effects on sleep timing don't meaningfully increase — but next-morning grogginess does.

Now check your bottle: it probably says 5 mg or 10 mg. US melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, so doses are set by marketing departments, not pharmacology. Analyses of melatonin products have found actual content ranging from ~-80% to +450% of the label claim — another reason third-party tested brands matter here more than usual.

**Practical fix:** buy 1 mg tablets (or liquid for titrating to 0.5 mg). More is not stronger; it's just more.

## The timing problem

Because melatonin is a clock signal, *when* you take it outranks how much:

| Situation | Sensible approach |
|---|---|
| Falling asleep too late (delayed rhythm) | 0.5–1 mg, **3–4 hours before** desired bedtime |
| Eastbound jet lag | 0.5–1 mg at local target bedtime, starting the evening before travel |
| Night-shift work | Strategically, per a doctor's guidance — this one's genuinely tricky |
| Ordinary "can't sleep" stress insomnia | Modest evidence; sleep-hygiene work first |

The common habit — popping 10 mg at midnight after failing to fall asleep — is the least useful possible deployment: too much, too late, for the wrong problem.

## Safety notes

- **Short-term use in adults is well tolerated.** Headaches and morning fog at high doses are the main complaints.
- **Kids and teens:** growing evidence of real use, but melatonin in children should involve a pediatrician, not a gummy brand's Instagram ads.
- **Interactions:** blood thinners (notably warfarin), immunosuppressants, diabetes medication, and contraceptives can interact — pharmacist check if any apply.
- **Pregnancy/nursing:** insufficient data; skip it.

## Bottom line

If you take melatonin, take **0.5–1 mg, timed for what you're fixing** — earlier in the evening for a late-shifted body clock, at target bedtime for eastbound travel. If it "never worked" at 10 mg, the dose wasn't the missing ingredient; the timing and the job description were.

For ordinary stress-related sleep trouble, the better-studied basics elsewhere on this site — magnesium glycinate, ashwagandha, and boring sleep-schedule discipline — earn their place first.
