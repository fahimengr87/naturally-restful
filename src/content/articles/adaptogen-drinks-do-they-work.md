The beverage aisle discovered stress. "Adaptogenic" sodas, sparkling waters, and lattes now promise to take the edge off your day for $3–5 a can. The marketing is seductive; the formulation math is less so.

## What's usually in the can

- **L-theanine** — typically 50–100 mg (the [research dose is 100–200 mg](/articles/l-theanine-for-a-racing-mind); so one can ≈ a half-dose)
- **Ashwagandha** — typically 150–300 mg of often *unstandardized* extract (trials use 300–600 mg of [standardized KSM-66 or Sensoril](/articles/ksm-66-vs-sensoril-ashwagandha-extracts))
- **Occasionally:** lemon balm, ginseng, or "adaptogenic blends" at homeopathic-adjacent doses

So the typical adaptogen drink delivers maybe half the theanine dose that works, a fraction of the ashwagandha dose that works, in unverified forms, for 10x the price of the supplement per active milligram.

## The two honest exceptions

1. **Theanine-forward drinks**, if they disclose 100+ mg, are roughly a capsule in disguise — fine if you enjoy the format and the sugar situation is sane.
2. **The ritual itself.** A non-alcoholic "wind-down drink" in the evening can genuinely help people replace beer number three. The benefit is behavioral, not botanical — and that's still a win worth having.

## The verdict

Adaptogen drinks are a lifestyle product wearing a supplement's lab coat. If stress relief is the goal, the [honest ranking](/best-sleep-supplements) — real doses of tested supplements — costs less per effective serving than a week of cans. If the *ritual* is the goal, enjoy the can, ignore the mythology, and check the sugar.
