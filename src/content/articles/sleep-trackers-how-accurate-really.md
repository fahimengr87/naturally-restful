Your watch says you slept 6 hours and 12 minutes, including 47 minutes of deep sleep. Here's the uncomfortable question: how would it know?

## The honest answer from validation studies

Sleep wearables (watches, rings, mattress strips) measure **movement and heart-rate variability**, then *estimate* sleep from those signals against population algorithms. Head-to-head comparisons with polysomnography — the EEG-based lab standard — consistently find:

- **Total sleep time: decent.** Most consumer devices land within 10–20 minutes for healthy adults sleeping normally. This is the number that's roughly trustworthy.
- **Sleep stages: not trustworthy.** Deep sleep and REM estimates from wrist devices agree with the lab poorly — often no better than a coin flip for any given night. That "47 minutes deep" figure is a model's guess, not a measurement.
- **Sleep onset and wake-ups: middling.** Trackers tend to *overestimate* sleep — lying still awake gets scored as light sleep. If insomnia is your problem, this is the failure mode that matters.

One line to remember: **trackers measure stillness and heart rhythms; sleep is a brain event.** The gap between those two things is where the errors live.

## Who trackers work for, and who they quietly harm

**Work for:** people with broadly healthy sleep who want trends — "did the late caffeine shift my average?" Trend arrows are more reliable than any single night's number.

**Harm:** people with insomnia, via a documented phenomenon called **orthosomnia** — anxiety about tracker scores that itself damages sleep. If you check your sleep score before your feet hit the floor, and a "72" ruins your morning, the device has become the problem. Studies and sleep clinicians now routinely recommend insomniacs *stop wearing trackers entirely*.

## The numbers worth reading, in order

1. **Your own trend line** (2+ weeks) for total sleep — ignore single nights.
2. **Bedtime consistency** — the most actionable thing a tracker shows.
3. **Resting heart rate trend** — a genuinely well-measured signal, and a decent proxy for recovery and stress load.
4. Anything about deep/REM percentages — **read as entertainment.**

## The verdict

A tracker is a fair *behavior* tool (schedule, consistency, RHR trend) and a poor *sleep* tool (stages, scores). If you use one, judge your sleep by how you feel and function, and let the watch report your habits — never the reverse. If you have insomnia and the morning score has become a referendum on your night, take it off for two weeks and see what happens to your anxiety.
