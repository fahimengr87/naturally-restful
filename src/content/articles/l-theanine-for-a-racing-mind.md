There's a peculiar fact hiding in your teacup: tea contains a decent dose of caffeine, yet tea drinkers report calm focus rather than coffee jitters. The main reason is L-theanine — and it's become one of the most-purchased calming supplements on the strength of that reputation.

Here's the honest breakdown of what it does, because "calming" is doing a lot of vague work in that sentence.

## What L-theanine actually does

L-theanine reliably increases **alpha-wave activity** in the brain — the pattern associated with relaxed wakefulness: alert, but not tense. This has been replicated since the late 1990s and is the supplement's best-established effect.

Translating that to real life, trials have found, at **100–200 mg/day**:

- Improvements in **subjective sleep quality** — falling asleep more easily, feeling more rested — notably in studies of stressed adults (Hidese et al., 2019, used 200 mg/day for four weeks) and in children with ADHD-related sleep trouble.
- Modest reductions in **stress and anxiety scores**, including blunted physiological stress responses (heart rate, salivary stress markers) under laboratory pressure.
- **No sedation.** This is the defining feature. Theanine doesn't force sleep the way a sedative attempts to; it removes the friction — the racing thoughts — that keeps you from drifting off naturally.

A 2022 systematic review of theanine and stress summarized the evidence as promising-but-modest: consistent direction, small-to-moderate effects, good safety. That's a fair reading.

## The caffeine pairing nobody markets correctly

Theanine's other well-studied use is **with caffeine** — roughly 100 mg theanine per 40–100 mg caffeine. The combo preserves caffeine's attention benefits while smoothing out the jitter and the crash-adjacent anxiety. If you're a coffee person whose 4 p.m. cup costs you 11 p.m. calm, this is the more interesting half of theanine's story.

## Who it fits — and who it doesn't

**Good fit:** your body is tired but your brain runs to-do lists at midnight; caffeine makes you anxious but you won't quit it; you want something non-sedating you can take during the day.

**Poor fit:**
- **Physical restlessness** (twitchy legs, tension) — that's more magnesium's territory ([our magnesium guide](/articles/magnesium-glycinate-vs-citrate-for-sleep) covers it).
- **Shifted sleep timing** (jet lag, night-owl clock) — that's melatonin's job.
- **Severe anxiety or insomnia** — see a clinician; a green-tea compound is not a treatment.

## Safety notes

L-theanine is about as benign as supplements get. Two footnotes: it can slightly lower blood pressure (relevant only if you're on BP medication — mention it to your pharmacist), and extremely high doses caused no meaningful harm in trials, which is why it has such a clean reputation.

## Bottom line

**100–200 mg in the evening** (or with your morning coffee) is the trial-backed pattern. Expect the volume knob on mental noise to turn down a few notches — not off. If after 3–4 weeks you notice nothing, it's not your tool, and the money is better spent on magnesium or [glycine](/articles/glycine-for-sleep-the-3-gram-dose).
