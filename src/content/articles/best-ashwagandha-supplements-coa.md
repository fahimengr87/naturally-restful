The ashwagandha aisle is the most scam-prone in the store — the herb's popularity attracted every corner-cutter in the industry. Studies analyzing commercial ashwagandha products have found everything from missing actives to adulterated material. This page is the antidote: exactly what a trustworthy bottle shows.

## Requirement 1: a named, standardized extract

Acceptable labels say one of:

- **KSM-66** — root-only, 5% withanolides, the [deepest trial record](/articles/ksm-66-vs-sensoril-ashwagandha-extracts)
- **Sensoril / ashwagandha (root & leaf), 10% withanolides** — fewer but solid trials
- **Shoden** — a newer 35% withanolide extract, early evidence, premium price

Reject: "ashwagandha root powder 500 mg+" with no standardization. That's agricultural product, not a supplement.

## Requirement 2: a real COA

The Certificate of Analysis (how to read one — [our guide](/articles/best-magnesium-glycinate-brands-coa)) should show:

- **Withanolide % matching the claim** (5% for KSM-66-class, 10% for Sensoril-class)
- **Heavy metals under limits** — especially **lead**; ashwagandha is a soil-concentrating plant and lead contamination is this category's most documented sin
- **No adulterants** — withaferin A spikes beyond expected ranges signal wrong plant parts

## Requirement 3: honest dose math

- KSM-66: **300–600 mg/day** — the trial range for stress and sleep ([dosing detail](/articles/ashwagandha-for-stress-what-the-research-says))
- Sensoril: **125–250 mg/day**
- And the exclusions don't move for any brand: pregnancy, thyroid, autoimmune — [the honest list](/articles/ashwagandha-side-effects-honest-list).

## Bottom line

Named extract + published COA + trial dose = a legitimate product. Anything else is a coin flip with your thyroid. Compare current tested brands via the category link; our [top picks page](/best-sleep-supplements) explains where ashwagandha fits among the options.
